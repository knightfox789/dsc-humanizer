# DSC Systems Transition Review

## Purpose

Use this skill when a task concerns:
- pilot-to-scale decisions;
- proof-of-concept review;
- programme scale-up or scale-out;
- government or institutional adoption;
- sustainability beyond the funded period;
- systems change or systems transition;
- water, agriculture, NRM or livelihood behaviour change;
- collective resource governance;
- donor or Governing Board strategy;
- programme redesign where local success has not yet translated into wider uptake.

The skill is designed to answer a specific question:

**Is this intervention structurally capable of becoming the larger change being claimed, and what must change for that to happen?**

Do not assume every project needs systems change. Some interventions scale mainly through disciplined replication. The first task is to identify the scaling pathway.

## Core rules

1. **Pilot success is not the same as scale readiness.**
2. **Do not use `scalable`, `institutionalised`, `sustainable at scale`, `state-wide`, or `systems change achieved` without a mechanism and evidence.**
3. **Scale pathways are context dependent.** Replication, production-system transition and collective-action transition require different designs.
4. **Training or awareness is not automatically a sufficient mechanism for livelihood or natural-resource behaviour change.** Economic incentives, risk, markets, policy, services and biophysical conditions may dominate.
5. **Collective-action systems may be limited by trust, negotiation and rule compliance rather than technical knowledge.**
6. **Easy, countable activities must not hide the make-or-break constraint.** Identify the rate-limiting condition.
7. **Separate programme contribution from changes already being driven by government, markets, technology, demography or other actors.**
8. **Be explicit about system boundaries, assumptions and evidence quality.**

## Review workflow

### Gate 1 — Define the intended scale

Establish what `scale` actually means in the case under review.

Specify, where available:
- target geography;
- target population or users;
- system boundary, such as village, aquifer, canal command, district, state, value chain or institutional system;
- intended time horizon;
- whether the objective is replication, institutionalisation, policy adoption, market transformation, collective governance or another form of scale.

Do not accept `scale` as a vague aspiration.

### Gate 2 — Classify the dominant scaling pathway

Classify the programme before judging it.

#### A. Replication / service-delivery pathway

Use when a reasonably standardised intervention can be repeated across locations with manageable adaptation.

Review:
- repeatability of the model;
- unit cost and staffing intensity;
- supply chain and logistics;
- training and supervision needs;
- quality assurance;
- local adaptation requirements;
- delivery capacity at larger volume.

A strong pilot may support replication if its operating model is realistic at the intended scale.

#### B. Production-system / incentive transition

Use when behaviour is tied to livelihoods, production decisions, economic return, risk or resource use.

Examples may include crop choice, irrigation behaviour, groundwater pumping, input use, natural farming, market-oriented production or conservation linked to income.

Test whether adoption is compatible with:
- income or net return;
- cost;
- labour and ease of implementation;
- risk and variability;
- input supply;
- market access and demand;
- finance and insurance;
- policy, subsidy, procurement or regulatory conditions;
- extension and technical support;
- biophysical feasibility.

Do not assume that a larger training target will overcome a misaligned production system.

#### C. Collective-action / trust transition

Use when success depends on groups agreeing, following and enforcing shared rules.

Examples may include canal management, aquifer governance, common-property resources, Water User Associations, cooperatives, FPOs, shared infrastructure or joint maintenance.

Review:
- trust and conflict history;
- incentives to cooperate or free-ride;
- rule-setting and enforcement;
- legitimacy of institutions;
- representation and inclusion;
- negotiation and facilitation capacity;
- mechanisms for grievance and dispute resolution;
- time required for repeated engagement.

Do not treat collective action as a fast individual behaviour-change campaign.

#### D. Hybrid pathway

Many water, agriculture and NRM programmes combine the pathways above. Separate the components explicitly:
- what can be standardised and replicated;
- what needs market/policy/incentive change;
- what depends on collective agreement and trust.

### Gate 3 — The scale-species test

Ask whether the pilot is designed to become the claimed larger programme.

Review whether local success depends on conditions that are difficult to reproduce, such as:
- unusually high subsidy;
- charismatic or exceptional leadership;
- unusually skilled project staff;
- very high facilitation intensity;
- free inputs or services that have no long-term financer;
- temporary donor-funded transaction costs;
- unusually favourable sites or participants;
- informal relationships that cannot be institutionalised;
- technology or data access unavailable at scale.

A high-performing pilot may still be a valuable pilot. If its operating model cannot plausibly grow into the intended system, describe that limitation directly.

### Gate 4 — Map current and desired system states

Create two explicit pictures:

**Current state / equilibrium → Desired state / equilibrium**

Then perform a structured `spot the difference` exercise.

Identify what must behave differently across relevant categories:
- users, farmers, households and community institutions;
- government agencies and frontline staff;
- markets, buyers, processors, input suppliers and service providers;
- finance, insurance and procurement systems;
- policy, regulation, subsidy and scheme design;
- infrastructure and technology;
- data, monitoring and decision systems;
- skills, extension and implementation capacity;
- norms, incentives, trust and collective rules;
- relevant biophysical conditions.

Do not list actors decoratively. State the decision, behaviour, capability, incentive or condition that must change.

### Gate 5 — Identify necessary, enabling and package conditions

Classify conditions as:

- **Necessary:** the transition cannot plausibly occur without them.
- **Enabling:** they improve the probability, speed or quality of transition.
- **Package / jointly sufficient:** the combination of conditions that would make the transition plausible.

Do not present one project activity as `the solution` when other necessary conditions remain unresolved.

### Gate 6 — External conditions and programme additionality

Identify changes already being driven by:
- technology;
- demographic or economic trends;
- climate or biophysical change;
- government programmes and schemes;
- regulation or infrastructure;
- private markets;
- other donors, NGOs or civil-society actors.

Then identify the **remaining gap** the programme is actually positioned to influence.

This is the programme's systems additionality.

Avoid claiming credit for system changes that would likely occur independently.

### Gate 7 — Find the rate-limiting condition

Identify the hardest make-or-break constraint.

Ask:
- What condition can block the transition even if easier activities succeed?
- Who controls or influences it?
- Is it inside or outside the programme's control?
- What evidence suggests it is tractable?
- Is the programme resourcing it adequately?
- What happens if it does not move?

Possible rate limiters include:
- policy approval;
- recurring finance;
- market demand;
- procurement;
- institutional ownership;
- frontline implementation capacity;
- trust and rule compliance;
- technical feasibility;
- data availability;
- infrastructure;
- user economics or risk.

Treat these as possibilities, not assumptions.

### Gate 8 — Build the scale architecture

A credible scale pathway should identify, where relevant:
- institutional owner;
- decision authority;
- recurring financer;
- delivery organisation;
- frontline capacity;
- supply chain and service ecosystem;
- supervision and quality control;
- data and monitoring system;
- policy and regulatory support;
- market and incentive alignment;
- learning and adaptation mechanism;
- collective governance or facilitation capacity;
- handover and accountability arrangements.

If these are unknown, state that scale readiness is incomplete.

### Gate 9 — Test resource realism

Compare the pilot operating model with the proposed scale model.

Check:
- staff intensity per site/user;
- subsidy per participant;
- transaction and facilitation cost;
- supervision load;
- data and technology cost;
- travel and coordination burden;
- institutional capacity;
- time required for adoption or trust-building.

Do not assume economies of scale will automatically remove high transaction costs.

For collective-action transitions, parallel local processes may be more realistic than one central process, but this requires distributed facilitation and negotiation capability.

### Gate 10 — Evidence and uncertainty

Classify important claims as:
- observed/descriptive;
- comparative;
- associational;
- causal;
- projected/modelled.

Distinguish:
- evidence that the pilot works under pilot conditions;
- evidence that the mechanism is understood;
- evidence that the model remains viable under wider conditions;
- evidence of actual institutional adoption or replication.

Do not convert a successful proof of concept into a causal or scale claim without additional evidence.

## Scale-readiness verdict

Use one of the following unless the user requests another format.

### GREEN — Ready for controlled scale
Use when the mechanism, ownership, resource model, critical conditions and rate-limiting constraint are sufficiently understood to justify expansion with monitoring.

### AMBER — Promising but conditional
Use when the pilot has credible results but one or more important scale conditions, owners, economics, system dependencies or rate-limiters remain unresolved.

### RED — Pilot success does not establish a viable scale pathway
Use when the proposed scale claim depends on unrealistic resources, missing institutional ownership, unresolved necessary conditions, a major uncontrolled rate-limiter, or a mechanism that cannot plausibly reproduce beyond the pilot.

### GREY — Insufficient evidence to judge
Use when the available record is too incomplete to assess scale readiness responsibly.

A RED or GREY verdict does not mean the pilot has no value. It means the evidence does not support the claimed pathway to scale.

## Required output structure

For a full review, return:

1. **Intended scale and system boundary**
2. **Dominant scaling pathway** — replication / production-system / collective-action / hybrid
3. **What the pilot has actually demonstrated**
4. **Current state → desired state**
5. **Necessary and enabling conditions**
6. **Existing external changes and programme additionality**
7. **Rate-limiting condition**
8. **Institutional owner and scale architecture**
9. **Resource realism**
10. **Evidence gaps / assumptions**
11. **Scale-readiness verdict** — GREEN / AMBER / RED / GREY
12. **Priority next actions** — normally 3–7 actions, ordered by dependency

For a short review, return the pathway, rate-limiter, missing scale mechanism, verdict and next decision.

## Domain-specific safeguards for water and NRM

For water, irrigation and natural-resource systems:
- respect hydrological or resource boundaries rather than only administrative boundaries when they materially determine the problem;
- distinguish infrastructure replication from governance change;
- distinguish individual conservation behaviour from collective aquifer/canal rules;
- distinguish technical water-saving potential from user incentives and rebound risks;
- distinguish creation of a committee from functioning collective governance;
- do not assume government convergence is secured merely because a scheme exists;
- test whether O&M, monitoring and recurring finance have an owner after project closure.

## Interaction with other repository skills

- Use root `SKILL.md` / `kaushal-voice` for final professional writing style if requested, but this skill owns the scale-readiness reasoning.
- Use `systems-effectiveness-tracking` when the question is how to monitor whether the transition, programme or wider system is actually improving over time. ## Final self-audit

Before finalising, verify:
- [ ] Scale is defined, not used vaguely.
- [ ] The scaling pathway is classified before recommendations are made.
- [ ] Pilot success has not been equated with scale readiness.
- [ ] Current and desired system states are explicit where systems change is claimed.
- [ ] Necessary conditions are distinguished from supporting activities.
- [ ] External trends/programmes are separated from programme additionality.
- [ ] A rate-limiting condition has been identified or explicitly remains unknown.
- [ ] Institutional ownership and recurring finance are addressed where relevant.
- [ ] Resource intensity is plausible beyond the pilot.
- [ ] Collective-action work is not reduced to training or awareness.
- [ ] The verdict matches the evidence rather than the ambition.
