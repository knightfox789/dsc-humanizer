# DSC Systems Effectiveness Tracking

## Purpose

Use this skill when a task concerns:
- programme effectiveness or organisational effectiveness;
- MEL / M&E design or review;
- donor or Governing Board performance frameworks;
- outcome and impact measurement;
- water-security or landscape indicators;
- intervention impact evaluation;
- cost per output, leverage, value-for-money or SROI discussions;
- deciding whether monitoring is sufficient to judge progress;
- long-horizon systems-change measurement;
- unintended consequences, rebound effects or trade-offs;
- designing dashboards or evidence systems for programme decisions.

The skill is designed to answer four different questions without mixing them:

1. **Is the organisation healthy and investible?**
2. **Is the programme using resources and producing outputs effectively?**
3. **Did the intervention work under the conditions tested?**
4. **Is the wider system or landscape actually getting better?**

These questions may require different indicators, evidence designs, time horizons and audiences.

## Core rules

1. **Start with the question and decision, not the indicator.**
2. **Do not treat organisational health, programme performance, intervention efficacy and wider system condition as the same thing.**
3. **Outputs do not prove outcomes; intervention outcomes do not automatically prove system improvement.**
4. **Match the measurement scale and time horizon to the expected mechanism of change.**
5. **Embed measurement in implementation when the effect cannot be reconstructed reliably afterwards.**
6. **For water and other dynamic biophysical systems, pre/post snapshots may be inadequate when seasonal, climatic or spatial variation matters.**
7. **Track material unintended consequences and trade-offs, not only the intended result.**
8. **Do not use a single composite metric to hide important dimensions that can move in opposite directions.**
9. **Cost-effectiveness, leverage and SROI require explicit definitions, attribution rules and assumptions; do not invent benchmarks or monetised benefits.**
10. **Data collection, cleaning, analysis, archiving, privacy, ethics and communication capacity are part of the measurement system, not free overhead.**
11. **Different funders and stakeholders may ask different questions, but do not infer their motivations from stereotypes. Use the actual grant, contract, governance or decision context.**
12. **If evidence is insufficient, say so rather than filling the gap with a more polished metric.**

## Measurement architecture

### Layer A — Organisational health and governance

Question:
**Is it safe and credible to invest in or partner with this organisation?**

Possible dimensions, where relevant and evidenced:
- reserves / liquidity;
- accounts payable and cash-flow exposure;
- statutory and donor compliance;
- audit status;
- annual reports and filings;
- Board independence, diversity and oversight;
- risk management;
- data/privacy/research-ethics compliance;
- transparency and documentation quality.

These indicators do not prove programme impact.

### Layer B — Programme efficiency and delivery

Question:
**Is the programme using resources reasonably to produce its agreed work?**

Possible indicators:
- cost per output;
- person-days per deliverable;
- timeliness;
- budget variance;
- delivery quality;
- utilisation of staff or assets;
- partner contribution / co-funding;
- unit cost trends;
- management burden;
- documentation and learning products completed.

Use cost-per-output indicators mainly for internal management and anomaly detection unless defensible external benchmarks exist.

A high cost per output may reflect complexity, novelty, geography, quality standards or learning investment. Do not label it inefficient without understanding the denominator and context.

### Layer C — Intervention effectiveness / MEL

Question:
**Did the intervention produce the intended outcome under the conditions tested?**

Review:
- theory of change / outcome hypothesis;
- baseline;
- treatment/comparison/control logic where appropriate;
- sample and site comparability;
- biophysical comparability for water/NRM work;
- measurement method;
- adoption or exposure level;
- causal/associational strength of the design;
- unintended effects;
- persistence of outcome after implementation.

Use the strongest design appropriate to the decision. Do not require an RCT where it is infeasible or unnecessary, but do not call descriptive monitoring causal evaluation.

### Layer D — External system / landscape condition

Question:
**Has the wider social, ecological or resource system measurably improved?**

This layer may include indicators outside the direct control of a project.

For water-security work, relevant dimensions may include, when appropriate:
- resource balance / availability;
- access and reliability;
- productivity / efficiency;
- resilience to variability or shocks;
- governance and institutional performance;
- ecosystem condition;
- equity or distribution where material.

Do not assume these dimensions can be collapsed into one number without consequence. If a composite index is used, preserve the sub-dimensions and make weighting/normalisation transparent.

## Review workflow

### Gate 1 — Identify who is asking and what decision they face

Record:
- stakeholder;
- decision to be made;
- time horizon;
- level of accountability;
- evidence standard needed;
- consequence of being wrong.

Examples:
- Board: organisational health and strategic performance;
- donor: compliance, outputs, outcomes, value or risk depending on agreement;
- programme manager: resource use, delivery bottlenecks and outcome progress;
- government partner: whether a model is worth adopting or expanding;
- community / public: whether conditions are actually improving.

Do not design one dashboard for everyone by default.

### Gate 2 — Map the results chain and measurement layer

Create:

**Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impacts / System condition**

For each proposed indicator, state which level it measures.

Flag:
- output indicators presented as outcomes;
- outcome indicators presented as system impact;
- organisation-level indicators used as evidence of programme success;
- external conditions attributed to the project without a mechanism.

### Gate 3 — Define the indicator question before the metric

For every important metric, write:
- What question does this metric answer?
- What decision could change because of it?
- What numerator and denominator does it use?
- What geography/population does it represent?
- What time period does it represent?
- What is the unit of analysis?
- What important behaviour or condition could it miss?

If the metric cannot answer a useful decision question, reconsider it.

### Gate 4 — Match time to mechanism

Ask when the effect should realistically appear.

Check:
- implementation season;
- hydrologic/agricultural cycle;
- wet vs dry years;
- lag between activity and outcome;
- persistence after project closure;
- time required for institutional or behaviour change.

Do not schedule an impact assessment solely around project start/end dates if the expected mechanism operates in another season.

### Gate 5 — Match spatial / system scale to mechanism

Ask where the effect should be visible.

Distinguish:
- plot / farm;
- household;
- village;
- aquifer;
- watershed;
- canal command;
- block/district/state;
- value chain or institutional system.

A farm-level improvement may be real without being large enough to alter watershed or aquifer condition. State both truths separately.

### Gate 6 — Decide whether continuous monitoring is required

Continuous or repeated monitoring is more important when:
- conditions change rapidly or seasonally;
- baseline/post measurements can miss the relevant period;
- cumulative effects matter;
- thresholds or extremes matter;
- the same intervention may perform differently under wet/dry or other variable conditions;
- rebound or displacement effects can emerge over time.

If continuous monitoring is required, budget for:
- sensors or repeated field measurement;
- data collection protocols;
- quality assurance;
- cleaning;
- storage and archiving;
- analysis;
- version control;
- privacy/ethics requirements;
- communication of findings.

### Gate 7 — Check unintended consequences and trade-offs

Ask:
- Can one user's gain reduce another user's access?
- Can efficiency improvements increase total use?
- Can one indicator improve while another deteriorates?
- Is impact displaced spatially or temporally?
- Are costs shifted to households, government, ecosystems or another group?
- Does the programme increase dependence on subsidy, facilitation or infrastructure?

For water, explicitly consider rebound/Jevons-type effects where efficiency may increase total use, and zero-sum conditions where one user's additional use can reduce availability to another.

Do not claim net system improvement until material trade-offs have been considered.

### Gate 8 — Evaluate cost, leverage and social value carefully

#### Cost per output
Use when the output definition is stable and cost allocation is credible.

Do not compare across programmes blindly when complexity, novelty, geography or quality differ.

#### Funding leverage
Define:
- catalytic funding amount;
- public/private funding allegedly unlocked;
- attribution rule;
- time window;
- whether the unlocked funding would likely have happened anyway;
- whether multiple organisations are claiming the same leverage.

Treat leverage as an influence indicator unless causal attribution is strong.

#### SROI / monetised social value
Use only where:
- the outcome is meaningful to monetise;
- valuation assumptions are transparent;
- deadweight, attribution, displacement and time horizon are addressed;
- uncertainty is reported.

Do not force SROI across every programme.

### Gate 9 — Assess the data system

A monitoring framework is not operational merely because an indicator list exists.

Check whether the organisation has:
- data owners;
- definitions and metadata;
- collection protocols;
- sampling plan;
- validation rules;
- data-quality checks;
- storage and backup;
- privacy and consent controls;
- analysis responsibility;
- reporting cadence;
- versioning and reproducibility;
- capacity and budget;
- archival rules.

Flag systems that collect data but cannot reliably clean, analyse, interpret or retrieve it.

### Gate 10 — Set expectation and attribution boundaries

State separately:
- what the programme controls;
- what it influences;
- what it merely monitors;
- what can reasonably be attributed;
- what can only be described as contribution;
- what remains external.

This is especially important for long-horizon systems work.

## Measurement-readiness verdict

Use one of the following when reviewing an existing framework.

### GREEN — Decision-ready measurement system
The questions, layers, indicators, timing, scale, data systems and interpretation rules are sufficiently aligned for the intended decisions.

### AMBER — Useful but incomplete
The framework can support some decisions, but material gaps remain in timing, scale, data quality, unintended consequences, attribution, benchmark logic or operational capacity.

### RED — Metrics risk giving the wrong answer
The framework relies mainly on activity/output counts, mismatched time/scale, unsupported attribution, misleading aggregation, missing trade-offs or an inoperable data system.

### GREY — Insufficient evidence to assess
The framework or implementation details are too incomplete to judge responsibly.

## Required output structure

For a full review, return:

1. **Stakeholders and decisions**
2. **Measurement layers** — organisational / programme / intervention / external system
3. **Results chain and indicator map**
4. **Time-horizon fit**
5. **Spatial/system-scale fit**
6. **Impact-evaluation design**
7. **Unintended consequences / trade-offs**
8. **Cost, leverage and SROI logic** where relevant
9. **Data-system readiness**
10. **Attribution / contribution boundaries**
11. **Measurement-readiness verdict** — GREEN / AMBER / RED / GREY
12. **Priority improvements** — normally 3–7 actions

For an indicator-design task, provide a compact indicator matrix with:
- decision question;
- result level;
- indicator;
- numerator/denominator or calculation;
- geography/unit of analysis;
- frequency/season;
- data source;
- owner;
- quality check;
- interpretation limit;
- action triggered.

## Water and NRM safeguards

For water, irrigation, watershed and groundwater work:
- align monitoring with hydrological seasons;
- distinguish rainfall, recharge, storage, groundwater levels, abstraction, allocation and reliable service;
- test whether plot/farm effects aggregate to the aquifer/watershed scale;
- consider wet/dry-year performance;
- use appropriate hydrologic comparison/control logic where feasible;
- track adoption intensity when system-level impact depends on coverage;
- consider rebound and redistribution effects;
- include governance and ecosystem condition where they are part of water security;
- do not infer water security from infrastructure creation or estimated water saving alone.

## Interaction with other repository skills

- Use `systems-transition-review` when the question is whether an intervention can scale or transform the system; use this skill when the question is how to track whether that transition is working.
- For scale programmes, the two skills should be used together: scale architecture defines what must change; effectiveness tracking defines how progress will be observed.

- CPC procurement review remains separate unless the task explicitly concerns programme efficiency or procurement performance indicators rather than a procurement decision.

## Final self-audit

Before finalising, verify:
- [ ] The stakeholder and decision question are explicit.
- [ ] Organisational health, programme performance, intervention effectiveness and system condition are not conflated.
- [ ] Indicators are mapped to activity/output/outcome/impact levels.
- [ ] Time horizon matches the expected mechanism.
- [ ] Spatial/system scale matches the expected effect.
- [ ] Continuous monitoring is considered where dynamic conditions require it.
- [ ] Unintended consequences and trade-offs are considered.
- [ ] Cost/leverage/SROI assumptions are transparent when used.
- [ ] Data collection is backed by an operational data system.
- [ ] Attribution and contribution boundaries are explicit.
- [ ] No benchmark, causal effect or monetised value was invented.
- [ ] The verdict matches the evidence rather than reporting pressure.
