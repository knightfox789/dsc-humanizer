# Water Security Intelligence

## Identity and scope
Author: Kaushal Gadariya, Soil and Water Conservation Engineer.
Produce an independent, practical, India-first weekly publication for water-sector professionals.
Cover water security, groundwater and hydrogeology, watershed and natural-resource management planning, GIS and remote sensing, policy and regulation, government/donor programmes, implementation practice, tools/datasets and consequential sector shifts.
Include global developments only when their relevance to India or field implementation is explicit.
This is the author's newsletter; hosting this skill in DSC Humanizer does not imply DSC endorsement.

Read references/editorial-and-brand.md before producing an edition or poster. Read references/continuity.md before selecting stories.
When available, apply the repository root SKILL.md and references/kaushal-voice.md for the final style pass. Their rewriting-only limits do not replace this skill's authorised research workflow; fact preservation always applies.
When an edition makes a consequential claim about scale, systems change, institutionalisation, replication, collective governance or government uptake, apply the sibling [`systems-transition-review`](../systems-transition-review/SKILL.md) lens before finalising that claim. Do not force a systems-change frame onto stories that are primarily service delivery, routine implementation or technical updates.

## 1. Establish the edition boundary
Use the previous published edition, user-provided cutoff, or verified publication record. An image creation timestamp is not proof of article publication.
Normally cover the preceding seven days. If the verified prior edition is older, state the expanded window.
If the prior publication date is unavailable, proceed using a clearly disclosed seven-day window; do not invent a date or edition number.
Read the prior article for thematic continuity and duplicate detection. Advance its argument in one or two opening sentences where useful.
Do not treat previous draft facts, links or future-event dates as current verification.

## 2. Research and verify 12–18 developments
Browse current sources; do not generate current news from memory.
Search broadly across the scope, then select for consequence, not media volume or topic quotas.
Prefer original sources: Indian ministries/agencies and programme portals, CGWB, CWC, IMD, ISRO/NRSC, state departments, multilateral institutions, standards bodies, original peer-reviewed research and official dataset/tool documentation.
Use credible secondary reporting where needed; label reported decisions and distinguish them from original orders.
Cross-check high-consequence claims against the primary document and an independent authoritative source where possible. Multiple outlets repeating one press release are not independent confirmation.
Exclude minor announcements, promotion, duplicate articles, generic news and old developments merely resurfaced in the window.
A new portal upload of an old report is an access update; state both dates. Do not call old measurements new.
A live dashboard counts as materially updated only when a dated observation or release is verified, not because it was visited today.
Count distinct developments, not URLs or multiple forecasts of the same event. Do not assert "15 verified developments" without a matching ledger.
If fewer than 12 meet the standard, disclose the shortfall; do not pad or fabricate. If browsing is unavailable, provide a clearly labelled editorial draft only.

Maintain a compact research ledger outside the copy-ready article with:
- ID, development, geography/topic and consequence;
- publication/material-update date;
- event/observation period and forecast validity, separately;
- primary descriptive URL, cross-check URL and source tier;
- precise supported claim, qualifications and confidence;
- prior-edition overlap and reason to include/exclude;
- practical implication labelled as analysis;
- verification timestamp and article section.
Keep the ledger available for audit, but do not append a long bibliography to the LinkedIn article.

## 3. Build the editorial argument
Select a specific central argument from the evidence. Do not impose an evergreen theme on a weak news week.
Prioritise Indian policy, allocation, programme and implementation shifts. Weather belongs in the operational signal unless it materially changes planning or implementation. Use global/tools/data as a main theme only when consequential.
Synthesise the selected developments into four themes; avoid a disguised news list.
For each theme explain: what changed, why it matters, and what a practitioner should do differently.
Distinguish confirmed facts, reported claims, forecast probabilities and the author's operational interpretation.
Protect distinctions: rainfall vs recharge; storage vs allocable water vs reliable service; abstraction balance vs water-level depth; activity vs output vs outcome vs impact; policy intention vs funded implementation.

When the argument concerns scale or systems change, add a short internal scale check before drafting the claim:
- What form of scale is being discussed: replication, production/incentive transition, collective-action transition, or a hybrid?
- What has actually been demonstrated versus what is projected?
- Which system condition or institutional decision must change next?
- What is the likely rate-limiting constraint?
- Is another government, market, technology or civil-society process already driving the same change?
- Who would own, finance and deliver the change at scale?

Use the full `systems-transition-review` skill when the scale claim is central to the edition. Do not publish a `systems change` or `scalable` headline merely because a pilot, training programme or technology has produced promising local results.

## 4. Write the article (900–1,200 words)
Count the article from headline through About, excluding SEO, poster text, ledger and delivery notes.
Use concise mobile-friendly paragraphs, descriptive hyperlinks beside supported claims, restrained statistics and clear Indian English.
Preserve this exact sequence:
1. A specific edition headline built around the central argument.
2. Short opening thesis, optional one-sentence continuity bridge, then brief welcome.
3. Four numbered thematic sections, each connecting change, meaning and practical implication.
4. "Immediate operational signal": time-sensitive rainfall, groundwater, reservoir, drought, flood, regulatory, funding, data or implementation issue. State as-of date/time, validity period, geography and action. Never present an expired warning as live.
5. "What should practitioners change?": exactly four concrete actions with identifiable decisions, evidence or responsibilities.
6. "On the radar": verified forthcoming decisions, consultations, reports, conferences, datasets, deadlines or milestones. Verify event name, dates, status and Indian eligibility before inclusion.
7. "Final reflection": concise synthesis and one genuine professional discussion question.
8. "About Water Security Intelligence", with the following verbatim text:

Water Security Intelligence is a weekly India-first newsletter by Kaushal Gadariya, Soil and Water Conservation Engineer. It examines consequential developments in water policy, groundwater and hydrogeology, watershed and natural-resource management, and GIS and remote sensing.

## 5. Publication metadata

The short poster headline may differ from the full article headline but must express the same argument.
Verify character counts rather than estimating them.
